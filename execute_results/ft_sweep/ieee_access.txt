Hierarchical Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Control for Electric-Vehicle Virtual Power Plants
in Balancing Markets
KOSHIN HAYASHI1, SIHUI XUE 1, DAISUKE KODAIRA 1
1Institute of Systems & Information Engineering, University of Tsukuba, Tsukuba, Ibaraki 305-8573, Japan
Corresponding author: Daisuke Kodaira (e-mail: kodaira.daisuke.gf@u.tsukuba.ac.jp).
ABSTRACTBalancing markets require flexible resources that can follow dispatch signals within tight
response-time budgets. Aggregated electric vehicles operated as a virtual power plant are promising for this
purpose, but practical deployment requires control architectures that can accommodate station-level available
power, user charging demand, and strict response-time requirements. This paper proposes a hierarchical
controller based on multi-agent deep deterministic policy gradient, in which each charging station is modeled
as an agent and trained under centralized training and decentralized execution. A case study motivated
by Japan’s balancing-market Frequency Restoration Reserve (FRR) product, hereafter referred to as the
Secondary 2 (S2) product, is conducted for a virtual power plant with five stations and 50 chargers. In a
representative single-training-run simulation, the proposed controller achieves a 97% dispatch tracking rate
within the allowable tracking-error band and an 80% target state-of-charge satisfaction rate when tested
on held-out dispatch trajectories. Practical latency feasibility is also discussed using a computation-time
analysis and measured communication/control-plane latency from a field demonstrator based on a distributed
energy resource remote-control platform used in Japan. The results indicate that the proposed architecture
is a promising alternative to fully centralized control for latency-constrained electric-vehicle virtual power
plants.

I. INTRODUCTION
W
ITH the increasing penetration of variable renew-
able energy, balancing markets are being established
worldwide to maintain supply–demand balance in power sys-
tems. In these markets, resources are required to follow dis-
patch signals within minutes, and settlement is based on track-
ing accuracy. Aggregated fleets of electric vehicles (EVs)
operated as electric-vehicle virtual power plants (EV VPPs)
are attracting attention as such balancing resources because
their fast, bidirectional charging and discharging capability
provides flexible distributed regulation.
However, controlling an EV VPP for balancing-market
participation still poses a difficult control problem. On the
one hand, the aggregate fleet output must track the dispatch
signal with high accuracy at every control step, which is a
dense fleet-level objective. On the other hand, each individual
EV user’s target state of charge (SoC) must be met by the
departure time, which is a sparse user-level objective. In
practical deployment, the control architecture must also re-
main compatible with low-latency operation and station-level
heterogeneity in available power due to site-specific intercon-
nection limits, feeder constraints, and concurrent loads [1],
[2].
Related studies can be organized into several strands, in-
cluding centralized optimization [3]–[6], centralized rein-
forcement learning (RL) [7]–[10], rule-based control [11],
distributed multi-agent RL (MARL) for EV charging [12]–
[15], global/local reward decomposition in MARL [16], [17],
and structurally related non-EV regulation problems [18]. As
summarized in Table 1, prior work already provides many of
the ingredients needed for the present problem, but not their
full combination. Closely related EV -fleet regulation studies
still rely on centralized information or hand-crafted logic,
while methodologically related MARL studies and non-EV
analogues do not address balancing-market EV -VPP control
with departure-time user-side fulfillment under a station-

deployment motivation and architectural consideration rather

theless, the station-based formulation is designed so that such
limits can be incorporated naturally in a deployment-oriented
extension.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows.
•Development of aggregator-to-station hierarchical
MARL control for EV VPPs under mobility con-
straints, and comparison with alternative control
methods.We formulate EV VPP control as a two-
level architecture consisting of an aggregator layer and
a station layer. The aggregator relays the TSO-issued
dispatch signal to all stations, and each charging station
is modeled as an agent whose policy determines charger-
level charging/discharging commands for its connected
EVs using only the broadcast dispatch signal and local
observations. The realized station power and fleet power
are the sums of these local decisions, enabling decen-
tralized execution without online global optimization.
The effectiveness of the proposed method is evaluated
against a centralized myopic LP controller, a centralized
single-agent SAC controller, and a fully decentralized
DDPG controller.
•A mixed local/global critic design for balancing-
market tracking and user-side charging demand.
Inspired by the decomposed-critic idea in DE-
MADDPG [19], we introduce a mixed-critic design
in which a local critic evaluates station-level user-side
charging quality and a global critic evaluates fleet-
wide dispatch tracking. Their gradient signals are com-
bined in the actor update to provide a structured credit-
assignment mechanism for the dual-objective EV VPP
setting.
•An S2-motivated case study with separate computation-
time and field-latency discussion.Using open data
and a simulation environment with 50 EV chargers, we
evaluate dispatch tracking and Target SoC satisfaction
under requirements relevant to the Secondary 2 (S2)
product in Japan’s balancing market. We then separate
aggregator-side online computation from information
and communication technology (ICT) / control-plane
latency, and discuss practical response-time feasibility
using field measurements obtained with a practical dis-
tributed energy resource (DER) remote-control platform
used in Japan.

B. SIMILAR METHOD, DIFFERENT PROBLEM
Distributed MARL for EV charging:Per-charger or per-
station distributed MARL has been explored for EV charging
networks [12]–[15]. Among these, Korkaset al.[12] are
closest to the present work in terms of learning structure:
they place one local DDPG agent per charging spot and use
a global coordinator that feeds back adifference reward. This
idea of separating global and local learning signals shares a
common philosophy with the mixed-critic design proposed
in the present paper. However, their study targets charging-
cost minimization and user satisfaction within a charging-
management setting; it does not address balancing-market
dispatch tracking against an external time-varying dispatch
signal. Shinet al.[13], Zhanget al.[14], and Wanget al.[15]
also investigate distributed MARL for EV charging, but not
the conjunction of per-step dispatch tracking and departure-
time fulfillment considered here.
Global/local reward decomposition in MARL:Beyond EV
charging itself, general MARL studies have proposed explicit
decompositions of global and local learning signals. DE-
MADDPG [19] considers settings where global and local
rewards coexist, while QPD [17] and IRA T [21] show that
team-level rewards create a credit-assignment difficulty and
that naive use of individual rewards can even be counterpro-
ductive. These studies support the methodological choice of
separating fleet-level and user-level learning signals. How-
ever, they do not address balancing-market EV -VPP control.

C. SUMMARY AND POSITIONING
In summary, the first three strands in Table 1 show that
closely related EV -VPP control objectives have already been
studied through centralized optimization, centralized RL, and
hand-crafted control, but each line retains a mismatch with
the present deployment setting: repeated online optimization,
dependence on centrally observed fleet-wide information, or
limited adaptability. The latter three strands show that dis-
tributed MARL, decomposed global/local learning signals,
and analogous fleet-versus-local trade-offs are already known
methodological ingredients, but not yet in the specific com-
bination required here. The present work is positioned at that
intersection: a station-based CTDE-MARL architecture for
balancing-market EV -VPP control that jointly addresses per-
step fleet-level dispatch tracking and departure-time user-side
fulfillment. Centralized RL is therefore included as a best-
effort comparator, and its limitations are examined experi-
mentally rather than assumed a priori.
III. CHARACTERISTICS OF BALANCING MARKETS
Many balancing markets require resources to respond to
operator-issued dispatch signals within short time budgets.
In this paper, the dispatch signal denotes the time-varying
regulation instruction issued by the transmission system oper-
ator (TSO) to a contracted balancing resource. For the present
control problem, it is modeled as a common external signal
that the EV VPP should track over time.
Balancing markets provide multiple product classes with
different response-time requirements. A recent review notes
that, among ancillary-service classes, EV flexibility is espe-
cially well aligned with secondary reserves in view of reserve
response times [22]. We therefore focus on the secondary
layer. Under the current Japanese product requirements, Pri-
mary reserve requires a 10-s response, Secondary 1 and
Secondary 2 both require a 5-min response, and Tertiary 1
and Tertiary 2 require 15-min and 60-min responses, respec-
tively [23], [24]. Within the Japanese secondary products,
Secondary 2 corresponds to frequency restoration reserve
(FRR) and can participate either via dedicated-line online
communication or via the simplified command system [23],
[24]. When the simplified command system is used, the
command interval is 5 min and the monitoring interval is 1
min [24]. Moreover, the simplified command system is based
on the common platform specifications developed in the
Japanese VPP demonstration projects [25]. Since the present
EV -VPP architecture assumes practical command broadcast-
ing from the aggregator to the station layer, Secondary 2 is
the Japanese product most directly aligned with the control
setting studied here.
Participation in such a balancing product requires not
only that a resource respond, but also that it track the in-
structed trajectory with sufficient accuracy. In Japan’s S2
product, a±10%band around the contracted value is treated
as an allowable tracking error band [24]. Because the S2-
motivated abstraction adopted in this paper evaluates per-
formance at the market-facing 5-min command interval,
communication/control-plane delay and device-side actua-
tion delay are not injected directly into the reinforcement-
learning environment. Instead, latency is treated as a prac-
tical feasibility requirement: whether the aggregate EV -VPP
output enters the allowable tracking-error band within 5 min
after a dispatch instruction is issued. Consistent with the
latency decomposition used later in this paper, this end-to-
end requirement is discussed by separating aggregator-side
computation from communication/control-plane delay and
device-side actuation/settling time [26].
In addition to grid-side tracking performance, EV -VPP
operation must satisfy user-side charging demand. This re-
quirement has been explicitly considered in prior EV -based
regulation and charging studies through EV -owner expecta-
tions, charging-time constraints, user charging anxiety, and
user requirements [5], [7], [9], [11]. Therefore, evaluating EV -
VPP participation only by dispatch tracking or latency would
be insufficient.
Accordingly, we assess EV VPP participation using (1) the
dispatch tracking rate, (2) user-side charging-demand fulfill-
ment quantified by the Target SoC satisfaction rate and the
average SoC shortfall, and (3) whether the total latency from
dispatch issuance to entry into the allowable tracking-error
band remains within 5 min.
IV. METHOD
A. OVERVIEW
We consider an EV VPP in which an aggregator bids the
aggregated flexibility of multiple charging stations into the
balancing market, as conceptually illustrated in Fig. 1. EV
users participate as long as their charging demand is satisfied,
and they receive compensation from the aggregator (e.g.,
discounted charging fees). In return, the aggregator earns
revenue from the balancing market by providing regulation
services through the aggregate response of the locally con-
trolled stations.
The proposed framework has a two-level control architec-
ture consisting of an aggregator layer and a station layer.
At the aggregator layer, the aggregator receives the dispatch
signal from the TSO and relays that same dispatch signal
to all stations. At the station layer, each charging station is
controlled by a station-level agent whose policy determines
charger-level charging/discharging commands for the EVs
connected to that station using only the broadcast dispatch
signal and local observations. The realized station power and
fleet power are the sums of these local commands; they are
not directly assigned by the aggregator.

C. BASELINES
We use three baselines: (i) a centralized myopic LP con-
troller, (ii) a centralized single-agent soft actor-critic (SAC)
controller, and (iii) a fully decentralized per-station DDPG
controller.
The centralized myopic LP baseline assumes full global
information and solves, at each time step, a centralized
single-step linear program using the current dispatch sig-
nal and the full EV state to compute per-charger charg-
ing/discharging commands. Slack variables represent residual
dispatch-tracking errors outside an allowable tracking error
band and unmet departure-time charge requirements, and the
objective minimizes a weighted sum of these slacks subject to
dispatch-tracking constraints, battery dynamics and bounds,
and per-charger power limits. This baseline is used as an
upper-bound feasibility reference under the simplified setting
rather than as a directly deployable low-latency architecture.
The centralized single-agent SAC baseline retains model-
free learning but removes decentralized execution. A single
centralized agent observes the full fleet state together with
the dispatch signal and outputs the joint charging/discharging
commands for all chargers.
The fully decentralized DDPG baseline assigns one in-
dependent DDPG agent to each station. Each station learns
and executes its own policy from local observations and the
broadcast dispatch signal only, without any centralized critic
or inter-station coordination, using a single scalar reward
formed by adding the model’s global reward directly to its
local reward.

dispatch tracking at the 5-min command interval, sub-step
latency differences are treated as a practical feasibility issue
rather than injected directly into the reinforcement-learning
environment.
E. IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
The proposed method was trained for 1,000 episodes with
a batch size of 1024 and a discount factorγ= 0.985. The
target networks were updated using Polyak averaging with
an update coefficientτ= 0.02. The learning rates were set
to2×10 −5 for the actor,1×10 −4 for the local critic, and
5×10 −6 for the global critic. The hidden-layer size for both
the actor and critics was 256.
In the actor update, the convex-combination weight was set
tow= 0.3. This value was selected by preliminary tuning
as a compromise between user-side charging quality and
fleet-level dispatch tracking: smaller values overemphasized
local charging demand and degraded tracking, whereas larger
values weakened learning of departure-time SoC satisfac-
tion. For exploration, we used a combination of Ornstein–
Uhlenbeck (OU) noise andϵ-greedy exploration, and the
exploration strength was decreased as training progressed.
The shaping reward was tuned to be approximately half of
the departure-time reward. The source code used for the sim-

the market discussion in Section III, S2 is adopted as the
Japanese target product for evaluating the proposed EV -VPP
controller. Because publicly reusable Japanese S2 dispatch
traces are not readily available, we use regulation-signal data
from PJM Interconnection (PJM) [28] as a fast-balancing
proxy. The PJM trajectories are linearly scaled to the bid
range adopted in the S2-motivated case study. Because the
allowable tracking error band in the S2 product is 10% of the
bid width [24], we set the allowable tracking error band to
±150kW.
This proxy choice preserves operational characteristics
relevant to the control problem considered here—frequent
up/down instruction changes, short dispatch intervals, and
continuous tracking requirements—but it does not imply that
PJM statistically reproduces Japan’s S2 dispatch signal ex-
actly. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as an S2-
motivated evaluation under PJM-derived fast-regulation tra-
jectories rather than an exact replay of Japanese S2 operation.
From this 31-day dispatch-signal dataset, 26 days are ran-
domly selected for training and the remaining 5 days are used
for testing. For the results reported below, one episode is run
on each of the five test trajectories, and the reported metrics
are averaged over these five test episodes.
Since dispatch signals are issued every 5 minutes under
the S2-motivated simplified-command abstraction [24], one
step of the Markov decision process (MDP) used in this
study corresponds to 5 minutes. One episode consists of
288 steps (24 hours). Communication/control-plane delay
and device-side actuation delay are not injected into this
RL environment; instead, they are evaluated separately as
feasibility factors at the same market-facing time scale. At
each step, a dispatch signal is provided, and meanwhile new
EVs arrive probabilistically at vacant chargers. Because pub-
licly available Japanese EV charging-session datasets with
the required fields (arrival SoC, Target SoC, dwell time,
and battery capacity) are difficult to obtain, we synthesize
the EV -side stochastic inputs by combining multiple open
datasets from different regions. No single public dataset used
here jointly provides all required fields in a form directly
usable for our simulation; accordingly, the simulation envi-
ronment does not reproduce real Japanese EV usage patterns
but instead constructs a plausible EV -charging scenario from
the best available international open data. Station-specific
arrival-time distributions are derived from public charging-
session records of five selected City of Boulder charging lo-
cations [29] by using the empirical distribution of session start
times for each station. In this study, these arrival distributions
are estimated from all available sessions at each station with-
out conditioning on weekday/weekend or holiday status. The
arrival SoC is sampled from the empirical distribution ofSOC
arrivalin the public dataset released by the Distributed
Electrical Systems Laboratory at Ecole Polytechnique Fed-
erale de Lausanne (DESL-EPFL) [30]. To construct user-side
charging demand, we jointly sampleminutesAvailable
andkWhRequestedfrom theuserInputsrecords of
the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) site in the Adap-
tive Charging Network (ACN) dataset [31]. The sampled
minutesAvailableis used as the dwell time. Because
vehicle-specific battery-capacity information required to con-
vert requested energy into a required SoC increment is
not consistently available across these sources, a nomi-
nal capacity of 50 kWh is introduced only for this conver-
sion. Specifically, the Target SoC is computed by adding
kWhRequested/50to the sampled arrival SoC, and the
resulting value is clipped to the range of 20%–80%. This
50 kWh value is used solely for Target-SoC construction and
is not the battery-capacity parameter used for the EV model
in the simulation.
To focus on the control problem itself, we adopt a par-
tially simplified simulation environment. Specifically, (i)
we assume that EV hardware parameters are homoge-
neous, with battery capacity 100 kWh and maximum charg-
ing/discharging power 27.5 kW; (ii) each EV departs at its
prescribed departure time, with no early or delayed depar-
tures; (iii) we control a total of 50 EV Level 2 chargers, with
10 chargers installed at each of five stations; and (iv) station-
level heterogeneity is introduced through station-specific em-
pirical arrival profiles, while charger counts and EV hard-
ware parameters are kept identical across stations. The bid
width is tuned so that the centralized myopic LP reference
achieves near-100% performance in both dispatch tracking
and departure-time SoC satisfaction, thereby serving as a
physically feasible upper-bound reference under the simpli-
fied assumptions.

C. BASELINE COMPARISON
Using the centralized myopic LP reference, we ran 10 trials
to average over dispatch-signal sets and stochastic EV ar-
rivals/departures. The dispatch tracking rate remained stable
at around 98%, and the departure-time Target SoC satisfaction
rate was around 99%. Because this controller assumes full
global information and centralized optimization at every step,
we interpret it as an upper-bound reference for the simplified
setting rather than as a directly deployable low-latency archi-
tecture.
We also trained a fully decentralized DDPG baseline.
Global and local rewards showed little growth; both dis-
patch tracking and the Target SoC satisfaction rate increased
slightly during training and then saturated. At test time, the
controller tracked only small-magnitude signals reliably, the
dispatch tracking rate remained near 40%, and the average
departure-time Target SoC satisfaction rate was around 40%.
For the centralized single-agent SAC comparator, best-
effort runs were also conducted. The centralized RL con-
troller achieved 61% dispatch tracking and 59% departure-
time Target-SoC satisfaction, which remained clearly below
the proposed MADDPG controller in both metrics.
Table 3 compares the four controllers in terms of Target-
SoC satisfaction, dispatch tracking, and computational fea-
sibility under large-scale deployment. The rightmost column
indicates whether each controller can complete its per-step
computation within a 3-minute budget when scaled to thou-

Optimization-based controllers such as LP and MILP have
clear advantages in constraint handling, interpretability, and
near-optimal single-step allocation when accurate system in-
formation is centrally available. However, they require cen-
tralized state collection and repeated online optimization, and
switching-aware MILP formulations can become computa-
tionally expensive as the fleet size increases. In contrast, the
proposed MARL controller sacrifices hard optimality and
constraint guarantees, but enables lightweight station-level
execution after training and reduces the need for online global
optimization.

COMPUTATION-TIME SCALING AND LATENCY
SEPARATION
The tracking and user-satisfaction results reported above are
obtained from the 5-min-resolution control model without
explicit communication-delay or actuation-delay injection.
Under the S2-motivated abstraction adopted here, such sub-
step latency is treated separately as a feasibility issue rather
than as a reward-shaping element. To assess practical feasi-
bility, it is therefore useful to separate aggregator-side on-
line computation from communication/control-plane latency.
The proposed station-based MARL controller requires one
forward pass per local actor at runtime and therefore scales
approximately linearly with the number of stations when the
actors are evaluated sequentially. By contrast, the centralized
single-agent RL baseline requires only one monolithic for-
ward pass. Centralized MILP is fundamentally different: once
switching-aware binary variables are introduced, the online
computation is dominated by branch-and-bound search and
can become prohibitively slow.
To quantify this difference, Fig. 7 compares the per-step
computation time of the switching-aware centralized MILP
controller and the proposed MADDPG controller as the num-
ber of stations increases (with the number of chargers per
station held constant). In the present implementation, central-
ized RL requires only a single GPU forward pass, whereas
MADDPG requires sequential forward passes ofNlocal
actors and therefore scales approximately linearly with the
number of stations. Even so, the MADDPG execution time
remains on the order of milliseconds at 100 stations. The
centralized MILP solve time, by contrast, grows rapidly with
the number of stations and reaches the 5-minute response-
time limit (300 s, shown as the dashed horizontal line) at
approximately 30 stations; beyond this point the solver is
terminated at the time limit, meaning that the solution quality
degrades or feasibility is lost.

B. LATENCY DEFINITIONS AND MEASUREMENT
METHODOLOGY
We evaluate latency components that are observable and ac-
tionable for real deployments. In the battery demonstrator,
the system provides both a control user interface that displays
instantaneous power and device states and a remote command
interface that changes operating modes and active power set-
points. In practical deployments, user-interface updates and
device logs can include buffering, polling, and asynchronous
processing. We therefore measure several latencies by direct
end-to-end observation using the user interface and device
indicators so as to capture the response as experienced by an
operator.
The latency components are defined as follows, consistent
with a decomposition into communication/processing delay
and device-side actuation/settling time [26]: state-to-display
latencyL disp, namely the delay from requesting a state or
instantaneous value to that value being displayed on the user
interface; set_mode-to-ack latencyL mode, namely the delay
from issuing a mode-switch command until its acknowledge-
ment or reflected state change is observed; state-query round-
trip time (RTT)L get, namely the delay from issuing aget
request until the state response is received or visible; and
set_power-to-reflection latencyL set, namely the delay from
issuing a power command until the measured output reaches
the commanded setpoint.

VII. DISCUSSION
The main limitation of the present controller is the Target SoC
satisfaction rate. Although the representative run achieves
strong dispatch tracking, the 80% Target SoC satisfaction rate
remains below the level desirable for practical deployment.
The main failure mode occurs when sustained discharging
commands near departure time override the user-side charg-
ing objective. This suggests that the present purely learning-
based policy should be complemented by a rule-based safety
layer that enforces charging recovery near the departure-time
constraint boundary.
A second limitation is the simplified simulation environ-
ment. The case study considers only five stations and 50
chargers; EV battery capacities and charger power ratings
are treated as homogeneous; station-specific available-power
ceilings are not explicitly modeled; switching constraints
are not enforced; and EVs are assumed to depart exactly at
their scheduled departure times. These simplifications were
adopted to isolate the core dispatch-tracking problem, but
richer heterogeneity and operational constraints should be
incorporated in future work.
VIII. CONCLUSION
This paper proposed an aggregator-to-station hierarchical
MADDPG-based control framework for low-latency EV
VPP operation in balancing markets. By integrating two
objectives—dispatch tracking and user charging-demand
satisfaction—within a CTDE-based multi-agent formulation,
we demonstrated a control architecture in which the ag-
gregator relays the TSO dispatch signal and station-level
agents autonomously determine local charger-level charg-
ing/discharging commands from station-local observations
and the broadcast dispatch signal. The aggregate fleet re-
sponse emerges from these local decisions rather than from an
explicit online power allocation computed by the aggregator.
The case study showed that the proposed method can
achieve high dispatch-tracking performance (97% tracking
rate), but the Target SoC satisfaction rate of 80% (with an av-
erage SoC shortfall of approximately 5%) remains a clear lim-
itation that must be addressed before practical deployment.
The study also clarified that the main architectural advan-
tage of the proposed method lies in reducing aggregator-side
online computation and avoiding online global optimization,
whereas overall practical response-time feasibility must also
account for communication/control-plane latency and device-
side actuation dynamics.
These results suggest that aggregator-to-station hierarchi-
cal MARL is a promising pathway for EV VPP control when
low-latency operation and scalability make fully centralized
control less attractive.
Future work will focus on two directions: (i) approaching
near-100% user satisfaction via a hybrid controller with a
rule-based safety layer, where the actor output is overridden
to command maximum charging only when the remaining
time and current SoC indicate that meeting the Target SoC
is physically infeasible; and (ii) extending the simulation
environment to include heterogeneous EV battery capacities
and charger ratings, broader station characteristics, explicit
station-level available-power limits, switching constraints,
stochastic departures, and larger-scale fleet experiments.
