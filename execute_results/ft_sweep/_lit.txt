II. LITERATURE REVIEW
Related work is summarized in Table 1. Rather than classi-
fying prior studies by an abstract comparison axis, the table
groups them into concrete research strands and states, for each
strand, what has already been achieved and what still remains
mismatched with the present setting. The detailed discussion
below follows the same logic: the first three strands are close
to the present problem setting, whereas the latter three are
methodologically related but address different problems.
A. SIMILAR PROBLEM, DIFFERENT METHOD
Centralized optimization:Problems close to ours have al-
ready been studied through centralized optimization. Rep-
resentative examples include day-ahead reserve bidding and
operation models [3], smart aggregation for secondary re-
serve [4], charging-time-constrained secondary frequency
regulation [5], and centralized MPC for multiple EV charging
stations [6], [20]. These studies show that EV aggregators can
provide reserve or regulation while accounting for user-side
charging requirements. The remaining issue is architectural:
such methods presuppose centralized collection of fleet-wide
information and repeated online coordination/optimization,
which becomes increasingly burdensome when low-latency
response and large fleets must be handled simultaneously.
Centralized reinforcement learning:Centralized RL has
also been applied to closely related EV -based regulation prob-
lems. Existing studies consider dispatch control for supple-
mentary frequency regulation under user expectations [7],
deep-RL-based V2G control [8], market-oriented regulation-
command disaggregation while balancing user charging anx-
iety and aggregate flexibility [9], and RL-based real-time
disaggregation of system-operator commands to EV charg-
ing stations [10]. Hence, it would be inaccurate to claim
